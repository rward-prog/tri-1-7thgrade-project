Percent change

Raw data is the number you measured, like 120 bpm. Percent change tells you how much that number changed compared to where it started. For measurements like heart rate and running time, it is often a fairer way to compare your conditions.

The formula

Percent Change = (After − Before) ÷ Before × 100

Part 1. Why percent change can be better than raw data

People start in different places. Your resting heart rate might be 60 bpm and your partner's might be 90 bpm. If you both rise by 30 bpm, the raw change looks the same, but your heart rate changed much more compared to where it started.

Maya+30
Jordan+30

Looks the same.

Percent change

Maya+50%
Jordan+33.3%

Now you can see the real difference. (Made-up data.)

The same goes for running time. Sam runs a set distance in 20.0 seconds and then 18.0 seconds, which is −2.0 s. Alex runs it in 30.0 seconds and then 27.0 seconds, which is −3.0 s. Alex's raw time dropped more, but both runners improved by the same percent: −10%. A negative percent for time means faster.

Percent change lets you compare fairly between partners, between days, and between people with different starting points.

Ask yourself: does every trial have a starting number (a baseline)? If yes, percent change is usually the fairer choice.

When raw data is fine: if there is no starting number, like counting sit-ups in 30 seconds or words remembered, use the raw numbers. You can also use raw data if everyone truly starts from the same number, but that is rare for heart rate.

Part 2. How to find percent change
  1. Find the change. After − Before.
  2. Divide by the Before number. This compares the change to where you started.
  3. Multiply by 100. This turns it into a percent.
  4. Keep the sign. A positive number means it went up. A negative number means it went down.
Heart rate: 60 to 90 bpm
1. 90 − 60 = 30
2. 30 ÷ 60 = 0.5
3. 0.5 × 100 = +50%
Running time: 20.0 to 18.0 s
1. 18.0 − 20.0 = −2.0
2. −2.0 ÷ 20.0 = −0.10
3. −0.10 × 100 = −10%

Mistakes to avoid

  • Dividing by the After number. Always divide by Before.
  • Subtracting in the wrong order. It is After − Before.
  • Forgetting × 100 on paper. (In Google Sheets the percent format does it for you, so do not multiply by 100 there.)
  • Averaging raw numbers from different people and then converting. Convert each trial first, then average the percent changes.
  • Forgetting to measure a baseline. You cannot find percent change without a Before number.
Part 3. Use it in your experiment
  1. Measure a starting number (baseline) before every trial. For heart rate, that is your resting heart rate. For running, do one normal run first. For reaction time, do a rested test first.
  2. Measure your ending number right after the trial.
  3. Make a table with Before, After, and % Change columns. Capitalize Important Words and put the units in the headers.
  4. Calculate the percent change for each trial.
  5. Average the percent changes for each condition, and graph those averages.
  6. Run your p-value on the percent changes, not the raw numbers. The P-value page has an AI prompt that does this when you give it your starting numbers.

In Google Sheets

  1. Put Before in column B and After in column C.
  2. In D2, type =(C2-B2)/B2 and press Enter.
  3. Click D2, then open Format, Number, Percent. Do not multiply by 100.
  4. Drag the small square in the corner of D2 down to fill the other trials.
  5. Average them with =AVERAGE(D2:D6).

More on tables and graphs is on the Sheets page.

Which measurements work well?

  • Use percent change: heart rate, breaths per minute, running time, reaction time, and balance time (each with a baseline).
  • Usually raw data: counts with no starting number, like sit-ups in 30 seconds, plank time, or words remembered. Ask your teacher if you want to compare them to a baseline.
