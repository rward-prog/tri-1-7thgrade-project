How to do each part

Open a step when you get to it.

1. Plan your experiment

Fill out the Methods Worksheet with your partner. Write two ideas, then choose one.

  • Independent variable (IV): the one thing you change on purpose.
  • Dependent variable (DV): what you measure. It must be a number, like beats per minute (bpm) or repetitions.
  • Controls: everything you keep the same. Think location, time of day, rest time, who is measured, and how you count.

Hypothesis format. Use If, then, because. Your "because" is where your one hypothesis source goes.

If I jog in place for 60 seconds instead of 30 seconds, then my heart rate will be higher, because working muscles need more oxygen, so the heart pumps faster (Source, Year).

Null hypothesis format. The null hypothesis says what you change makes no difference to what you measure. You will test it with your p-value.

There will be no difference in heart rate between 30 seconds of jogging in place and 60 seconds of jogging in place.

How many trials? You need 10 data points in all. A simple split is 5 trials of Condition A and 5 trials of Condition B. Be ready to explain why you chose your number.

Writing a research question (with examples)

Your research question is the one question your experiment answers. Use this pattern:

Does ______ (what you change) change ______ (what you measure)?

You can also ask "Does ______ raise (or lower) ______ more than ______?"

A strong research question:

  • Names the one thing you change (independent variable).
  • Names the thing you measure (dependent variable), with a number you can collect.
  • Compares two conditions, like 30 seconds vs 60 seconds.
  • Can be tested with materials you can get.
  • Does not have an obvious answer before you test.

Strong questions

  • Does jogging in place for 60 seconds raise heart rate more than jogging for 30 seconds?
  • Does slow deep breathing for 2 minutes lower resting heart rate more than sitting quietly?
  • Does listening to fast music change the number of squats a person can do in 1 minute?
  • Does a 1 minute warm-up change how many push-ups a person can do in 30 seconds?
  • Does exercise right before a memory test change how many words a person remembers?

Needs work, and how to fix it

Is exercise good for you?Too broad. You cannot answer it with one measurement.Does 1 minute of jumping jacks raise heart rate?

Stuck? Pick your independent variable and your dependent variable in the Experiment builder. It writes a research question for every pair. You can also see these examples on the Rubrics page and in the self-check.

2. Collect your data
  1. Gather every material on your list before you start.
  2. Measure resting heart rate first. Count the pulse for 15 seconds and multiply by 4, or use a watch or timer.
  3. Write down your starting number (baseline) before every trial. You will use it to calculate percent change, which is often fairer than raw numbers. See the Percent change page.
  4. Run each trial the same way every time. Use the same pace, the same rest time, and the same counting method.
  5. Write each result in your data table right away. Do not wait until later.
  6. Rest fully between trials so one trial does not affect the next.
  7. Take a photo of your setup for your slides.

Split the jobs: one partner does the activity, the other times and records. Switch roles when you can.

3. Materials and Methods presentation (5 minutes max)

Start from the Materials and Methods slides template. Put a picture on every slide and keep the words short. You talk, the slides support you. Cover these in order:

  1. Your experiment in one sentence, and your research question.
  2. What you change (independent variable), what you measure (dependent variable), how many trials you ran, and why that number.
  3. Your hypothesis (If, then, because).
  4. Your null hypothesis (no difference).
  5. A bulleted materials list.
  6. Your procedure, step by step, including how long you rested between trials.
  7. Your data table layout. Show the labeled columns with units. No numbers in the cells yet.
  8. What you control, and what you cannot control and why.
  9. Your timeline and plan.

Both partners must speak about equally. The Materials and Methods rubric below shows how it is graded.

4. Make your table, make your graph, and analyze your data

Data table:

  • Capitalize Important Words in every header.
  • Put the units in the headers, like (bpm) or (Seconds).
  • Use alternating colors and outline every cell in Google Sheets.
  • If you have a starting number, add Before, After, and % Change columns. See the Percent change page.

Graph:

  • Check that you are using the right graph: columns to compare conditions, a line for change over time, a scatter chart for two numbers.
  • The title and both axis titles need Capitalized Important Words and the units on the axes.

Step-by-step help for all of this is on the Tables and graphs page. Then use the AI prompt builder on the P-value page to get your averages and p-value, and check them yourself.

Part F helps you get ready to present. Parts G and H help you look back at your whole project and the whole trimester. It is on the Downloads page and is due Monday 11/16.

6. Final presentation outline

Start from the Final presentation slides template. It follows this order and has a Read This First slide with reminders. Capitalize every slide title.

  1. Title and Partners
  2. Background: Source 1, with a "What This Means" for your experiment
  3. Background: Source 2, with a "What This Means" for your experiment
  4. Research Question
  5. Hypothesis (If, then, because)
  6. Materials and Methods (variables and controls)
  7. Recap of the Experiment (one slide: question, IV, DV, conditions, trials, hypothesis)
  8. Results: data table and graph together on the same slide
  9. Data Analysis and P-Value
  10. Conclusion: Was your hypothesis supported?
  11. Errors and How You Would Fix Them
  12. Future Expansion
  13. References in APA format

Make it a story, not a checklist. Start with why anyone should care. End each slide with a sentence that leads into the next one, like "That is why we tested...", "So here is what happened...". Finish with what it means. Do not just follow the rubric in order.

Your final presentation is 8 minutes or less. Practice out loud with a timer. Do not read your slides. Expect questions from the audience. Use the self-check before you present.

7. Writing about errors (no "human error")

"Human error," "we messed up," and "we were not careful" do not count. Instead, find something in your design that could have changed your numbers. Then say how you would change the experiment.

Good places to look:

  • How you measured: Counting a pulse for 15 seconds and multiplying by 4 means one missed beat changes your answer by 4 bpm.
  • Timing: Heart rate drops fast after exercise, so a few seconds of delay finding your pulse changes the number.
  • Things you could not control: time of day, sleep, what you ate, stress, room temperature, or doing trials in different places.
  • Small sample: With 5 trials per condition, one odd result can move the average a lot.
  • Order: If Condition B always came second, your body may have been warmer or more tired.
  • Equipment: a phone stopwatch, different floors, or different music volume.
Sentence frame: Because a specific problem in the design, my results may how it could change the data. Next time I would a specific change to the experiment.
8. Slide rules that cost points
  • Every image must be about your experiment. Use your own photos of your setup and trials, your own graphs, or a labeled diagram of what you did. Do not use GIFs, memes, or pictures you found by searching for your hypothesis.
  • Label AI images. If an image was made with AI, write "AI-generated" under it.
  • Capitalize your slide titles. Use Title Case on every slide.
  • Keep your citations consistent. Use the same APA style on your slides and on the References slide.
  • Put your table and graph on the same slide.
  • Do not use "human error" as an error. See step 7.
